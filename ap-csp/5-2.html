BIG IDEA 5: IMPACT OF COMPUTING · TOPIC 5.2

5.2 Digital Divide

Not everyone has the same access to computing. The exam wants the causes, the consequences, and the recognition that it happens both between and within countries.

What you need to know

  • The digital divide is the gap between people who have reliable access to computing devices and the internet and people who don't.
  • Contributing factors: socioeconomic status (cost of devices and service), geographic location (rural areas without infrastructure), demographics (age, disability, education), and government policy (censorship, investment).
  • The divide exists between countries and within a single country — including within a single city.
  • Consequences compound: as school, jobs, healthcare, banking, and government services move online, lacking access means being excluded from essential parts of society.
  • Access isn't only devices and bandwidth — it includes digital literacy (knowing how to use technology) and the availability of content in one's language.
  • Actions that reduce the divide: public infrastructure investment, low-cost devices, community access points (libraries), and designing software to work on older devices and slow connections.
  • Developers contribute to the divide when they assume all users have fast connections and new hardware.

Worked example

During remote schooling, two students in the same district get the same assignments. One has home broadband and a laptop; the other shares a phone with two siblings and uses a fast-food parking lot's Wi-Fi. Same curriculum, wildly different outcomes — that's the digital divide operating within one town. A school that lends laptops and pays for hotspots is acting to narrow it.

Exam tip: The digital divide is about access, not attitude. Options describing people who "choose" not to use technology are distractors. And it's not only an international issue — a question set entirely within the US can still be about the digital divide.

Going deeper

The nuance, edge cases, and connections that turn a 3 into a 5.

  • The digital divide is unequal access to computing and the internet. The CED names causes: socioeconomic status, geographic location, demographic characteristics (age, disability, education), and government or institutional policy (censorship, lack of investment).
  • It exists at every scale: between countries (broadband penetration varies enormously), within countries (rural vs. urban), within cities (neighborhood by neighborhood), and within households (who gets the one device).
  • The divide is about more than hardware: digital literacy (knowing how to use technology), affordability of ongoing service (not just a device), reliability of connection, language (most content is in a few languages), and accessibility for people with disabilities are all dimensions.
  • Why it matters more over time: as essential services move online — school, job applications, healthcare portals, government benefits, banking — lacking access stops being an inconvenience and becomes exclusion from society's basic functions. The divide compounds other inequalities.
  • Developers contribute to the divide when they assume their users are like them: fast connections, new devices, large screens, fluent English. A site that requires 50 MB of JavaScript excludes people on slow connections. This connects to 5.3 (bias) and to 1.3 (investigating users during design).
  • Actions that reduce the divide: public infrastructure investment, subsidized service, community access points (libraries, schools), low-cost devices, and — for developers — building things that work on old devices and slow connections, in multiple languages, with accessibility features.
  • The digital divide can also refer to generational differences: older people may have less familiarity even with access. And it can be policy-driven: some governments restrict internet access deliberately.

Mistakes that cost points

  • Treating it as only an international issue. Questions set entirely in one country — even one city — can be about the digital divide.
  • Treating it as a matter of choice. Someone who prefers paper isn't part of the digital divide. Someone who can't afford broadband is.
  • Assuming a device solves it. A laptop with no internet, or internet with no skills to use it, doesn't close the gap. It's multi-dimensional.
  • Picking options that widen the gap. "Require the latest phone," "raise prices," "use high-resolution video only" — these are wrong answers to "how would you reduce the divide."

Practice questions

Written in the style of the real exam. Try each one before revealing the answer.

Q1 Which of the following is an example of the digital divide?
  1. A Two students use different brands of laptop.
  2. B Students in a rural area lack broadband access and cannot complete online homework, while students in a nearby city can.
  3. C A student prefers paper books to e-books.
  4. D A website is available in multiple languages.
Show answer

Answer: B. Unequal access based on geography that affects opportunity is the definition of the digital divide.

Q2 Which of the following actions would most directly help reduce the digital divide?
  1. A Requiring all websites to use high-resolution video
  2. B Providing free public internet access and affordable devices in underserved communities
  3. C Increasing the price of internet service
  4. D Designing apps that only run on the newest phones
Show answer

Answer: B. Reducing cost and infrastructure barriers directly expands access. The other options widen the gap.

Key vocabulary

Digital divide
the gap between those with reliable access to computing and the internet and those without
Digital literacy
the skills needed to use computing technology effectively
